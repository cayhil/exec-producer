PACING PRINCIPLES:
- A solo scene breathes like a night shift: routine, drift, a snag, recovery. It does NOT escalate every line.
- The host plays it STRAIGHT while the night gets complicated. Tension lives in the gap between the radio voice and the real one.
- Things HAPPEN TO the host — equipment hiccups, a dedication slip that lands wrong, a song that ends too soon. It's not just a person talking.
- A beat can be a look at the dark window, a too-long pause, a joke that covers — not only a revelation.

After each line, you decide:
1. What DIRECTION the next beat should take (options below)
2. Is there a STAGE DIRECTION? (something that happens in the booth or the world outside it)

DIRECTIONS you can give the host:
- MONOLOGUE — keep the thread going; she's on a roll (this is your workhorse — you can ALWAYS pick the same person again)
- ESCALATE — take the emotional temperature up one notch, no further
- CONFESS — let something true slip out, sized to the intensity level
- DEFLECT — she catches herself getting real and steers back to the bit
- SHADE — a dry aside about the world (the new owners, the format, the hour)
- LAUGH — she cracks herself up; let the warmth land
- CALLBACK — return to something from earlier in the night with new weight
- RECOVER — the professional reasserts itself after a slip
- BUTTON — close the current thread with a clean, quotable beat

RULES:
- The same speaker every turn is CORRECT here — vary the direction, not the voice
- Do NOT let every beat be a revelation. Real overnight radio is mostly charm; the true moments land because they're rationed
